01Context

Scientific abstracts are long, dense and full of specialised vocabulary. The project explored a corpus of abstracts on women's health, with no labels describing their topics.

02Question

Can an unsupervised pipeline recover interpretable research themes from biomedical abstracts?

03Obstacle

Word-count representations such as TF-IDF capture vocabulary, not meaning: two abstracts on the same topic can share few words. And without labels, there is no ground truth to say whether a clustering is right.

04Choices

How should the texts be represented?methodological question

Tension
Word counts miss meaning in long technical texts.
Choice
SciBERT embeddings, a language model pre-trained on scientific papers.

How many clusters, and with which settings?methodological question

Tension
Clustering results depend heavily on hyperparameters, and there are no labels to tune against.
Choice
HDBSCAN, which does not need a fixed number of clusters and can leave points as noise, tuned by Bayesian optimisation on an internal validity score.
05Work
  1. Abstracts
  2. SciBERT embeddings
  3. UMAP reduction
  4. HDBSCAN clustering
  5. Interpretation
06Checks
  • Density-based validity score (DBCV ≈ 0.5).
  • Share of abstracts left unclustered (about 17 %), reported rather than hidden.
  • Manual reading of each cluster to check that it forms a coherent theme.
07Outcome

Four themes emerged: cancer and survival, maternal health and pregnancy complications, reproductive health and genetics, and contraception and access to care, plus a noise group.

08Limits

What remains open

  • Topics overlap in the literature, so cluster boundaries are not sharp.
  • Without labels, the result is an interpretable map, not a classification to be scored.

What I took from it

  • Choose the representation for the meaning of the data, not for convenience.
  • Report the noise and the uncertainty as results in their own right.
